How many New York renters are cost-burdened, and where?

A ZIP-code-level look at renter households that spend 30% or more of their income on gross rent, alongside the City's affordable housing production record.

Burden is a spectrum, not a line

Share of renter households in each burden bracket. Brackets above 30% of income stack to the borough's rent-burden rate; the darkest segment (50%+) is severe burden.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2024 5-Year Estimates, Table B25070, aggregated from ZCTAs. Denominator excludes households for which burden is "not computed".

Rent burden rate

Renter households paying ≥30% of household income on gross rent ÷ renter households for which the ratio is computed.

Severe rent burden

The subset paying ≥50% of income on gross rent, over the same denominator.

Affordable housing production

HPD "counted" affordable units in projects started since 2014. A measure of policy activity, not of need or of current affordability.

Pressure indices (custom)

Custom research indices built in Homework 4, not official statistics. They give a relative 0–100 ranking among NYC ZIPs, not absolute levels, and do not imply cause and effect.

Reading these data

  • Survey estimates vs. administrative counts. Rent burden, renter households and income are ACS survey estimates with sampling error (shown as 90% margins of error). HPD affordable units are administrative counts, which have no sampling error but can be revised.
  • Different reference periods. ACS 2024 five-year estimates pool the survey years 2020–2024. HPD activity covers projects started from 2014 to mid-2026.
  • What HPD counts. Counted units include both new construction and preservation of existing homes, and both rental and homeownership units.
  • ZIP codes ≠ ZCTAs. Census ZIP Code Tabulation Areas approximate, but are not identical to, USPS ZIP codes; HPD records are matched on their postal ZIP.
  • Association, not causation. Comparisons between production and burden are descriptive. They do not show that low production causes high burden, or that more production would reduce it.

Affordable housing

Affordable housing production

HPD's production data records what the City financed or preserved — it is a flow of policy activity. Rent burden is a condition of households at a point in time. The two are measured on different populations and timelines, so a ZIP's production level says nothing by itself about whether burden there rose or fell.

Affordability pressure

Where high burden meets low housing activity

The affordability pressure index from Homework 4 combines two ranks: how high a ZIP's rent burden is, and how little affordable housing activity HPD has recorded there. It is a custom, relative research indicator — a ranking among NYC ZIP codes, not an official statistic and not a measure of cause and effect. The policy simulator shows how this ranking responds to hypothetical increases in housing activity.

What if affordable housing activity increased?

The sections above are descriptive: they show where rent burden is high and where recorded affordable housing activity is low. This tool adds a prescriptive, scenario-based view. Simulation — not a causal forecast. Rent burden is held fixed at its 2020–2024 ACS estimate. The tool shows how the HW4 index would re-rank ZIP codes under a hypothetical change in recorded activity. It does not predict rents, household burden, or the effect of any policy.

How the simulation works and what it assumes

  • Same formula as Homework 4. The index is recomputed in your browser from the published counts: rank of rent burden (high→low) plus rank of HPD units per 1,000 renter households (low→high), rescaled to 0–100. The page checks that its current values match the published HW4 index exactly.
  • What changes: only the activity measure of the selected ZIPs, which increases by the chosen number of units per 1,000 renter households. The renter household denominator is the ACS 2020–2024 estimate.
  • What does not change: rent burden, incomes, rents and renter households. The tool assumes no effect of new units on burden, because the data cannot tell us what that effect would be.
  • The index is relative. Improving some ZIPs moves others up the ranking even though nothing changed there, and a uniform increase everywhere changes nothing. Use the change map to see this re-ranking.
  • Scale. "Hypothetical additional units" multiplies the increase by each ZIP's estimated renter households ÷ 1,000. It is a size reference, not a cost or feasibility estimate. HPD counts both new construction and preservation.
  • Uncertainty. ZIP rankings inherit sampling error from the ACS estimates. Small ZIPs (⚠) can move a lot for statistical reasons alone.
